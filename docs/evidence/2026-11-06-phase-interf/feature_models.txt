Study F1: bandwidth that drifts continuously
  emitter 1: 1 bin widening by d bins per frame at 10 deg; emitter 2:
  5 bins at -30 deg, fixed. 8 frames, 16 snapshots, 6 draws.
  Both rules get the same widened gate for a track's first two frames, so the
  only difference is the drift model. A fixed cost is |bins - mean|
  against a +-1.5 bin gate; the modelled one predicts the drift.
  drift             fixed       modelled        ignored
  bins/frame           kept           kept           kept
  0.0                100%           100%           100%
  0.5                 50%           100%           100%
  1.0                 38%           100%           100%
  2.0                 38%           100%           100%
  A bandwidth that changes faster than the gate breaks a fixed cost
  (38% at 2.0 bins/frame) and the rate model restores it to 100%.
  Dropping the term also works, because the direction still separates
  the two emitters (100%): a feature is only worth a gate when it is
  needed to separate somebody.

Study F2: bandwidth that jumps between two modes
  emitter 1 hops to a new carrier every frame AND alternates 1 and 4 bins at
  10 deg; at frame 4 a static 6-bin newcomer appears at the SAME 10 deg.
  Only the bandwidth gate and the cadence can tell them apart.
  rule                           dipper      newcomer     tracks
                                   kept      absorbed           
  gate +-1.5 bins                   50%            0%        3.0
  gate +-2.5 bins                   50%           12%        3.0
  gate +-4 bins                    100%            0%        2.0
  gate +-4 bins + cadence           92%            4%        2.2
  So a jumping feature cannot be a key, and tolerating it with a wide
  gate leaves a track whose bandwidth is the mean of two modes. What
  that costs is measured next: a newcomer appears at frame 2 with a fixed
  carrier, and the question is whether it ends the run in a track of its
  own.
  newcomer bins      gate +-1.5   gate +-4   cadence, +-4
                                           (ends in own track)
  2 bins                   100%        42%           100%
  4 bins                   100%        75%           100%
  6 bins                   100%       100%           100%
  8 bins                   100%       100%           100%
  Tolerance and discrimination are the same knob: after eight frames the
  +-4 bin gate has swallowed the 2-bin newcomer (it owns its track in
  42% of runs against 100% with the +-1.5 gate), and the narrow
  gate that avoids it is the one that shreds the dipper. Cadence is
  late, not absent: the newcomer is swallowed at arrival like any other
  cluster, and once its carrier has repeated a few times it stops
  matching a hopping track (100% own track with cadence, at the end).
  A pattern needs frames to become a pattern; a value needs none.

Study F3: a direction that jumps instead of moving
  emitter 1 sits at -20 deg and steps by one jump at frame 4; emitter 2
  is 40 deg away. First: is the jump absorbed by the track?
  jump       gate 4 deg     gate 8 deg    gate 16 deg
  5 deg             50%           100%           100%
  10 deg            50%            50%           100%
  20 deg            50%            50%            50%
  40 deg            50%            50%            50%
  Then the price of a wide gate: a neighbour sits exactly at the landing
  point, and the question is how confused the two tracks become.
  jump        confused at 8 confused at 16
  10 deg                25%             0%
  20 deg                12%            25%
  40 deg                25%            25%
  A jump is not motion: at a 4 deg gate the track splits in two (50%
  of the emitter in one piece) and a gate wider than the jump absorbs
  it (100% at 16 deg for a 10 deg jump).
  Confusion is the price of the wide gate: 0-25% of the detections
  end in the other emitter's track once the gate admits the jump. It
  does not fall again with a wider gate, because after the jump the two
  emitters occupy the same direction with the same bandwidth -- the
  crossing limit of V07 14.6, arriving from a jump instead of a walk.
  That is why the gate has to be a speed limit from the physics, not a
  tuning knob: every degree of gate is a degree of identity risk.

Study F4: direction and bandwidth changing at the same time
  emitter 1: +2 deg/frame and +0.5 bins/frame; emitter 2 fixed at -30
  deg with 5 bins. Which model keeps the track?
  rule                                     kept     tracks
  direction only                            50%        3.7
  direction + bandwidth, fixed              38%        4.3
  direction + bandwidth, both modelled       100%        2.0
  Both terms drift and both have to be modelled, or dropped: with the
  bandwidth fixed the track dies as soon as the width leaves the gate,

VERDICT
  You cluster on features whose model matches how they change. For each
  feature, the question is one ratio: how far does it move in one frame
  compared with how well it is measured (about 1 deg and one bin here)?
  feature                    changes by             so it is
  direction      0-16 deg/frame measured           rate model
  bandwidth      0-2 bins/frame, or jumps         rate or mode
  carrier         up to 800 MHz per hop         pattern only
  level              several dB per hop             not used
  1. CONTINUOUS change (a mover, a chirp): model the rate and predict;
     the gate stays at its measurement-limited width (F1, F4).
  2. DISCONTINUOUS change (a hop, a mode change): the value jumps, so it
     cannot be a position key. Widen the gate and it becomes a bad key
     (F2: merged detections grow with the gate), so use its *pattern*
     over a window instead -- cadence -- or drop it.
  3. FAST change (level, coherence under fading): no model saves it; it
     never enters the cost.
  Concretely, association is: (direction with a rate) + (bandwidth with
  a rate, or out of the cost) + (cadence, which is a pattern, not a
  value) -- and a jump is a new track, not a fast target.
