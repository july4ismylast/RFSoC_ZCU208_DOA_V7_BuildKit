Clustering emitters in the (frequency, azimuth, time) cube
seed 20261108, 6 draws, 410 bins of 1.9526 MHz over 2.70-3.50 GHz
Simulated with decoy_discrimination.simulate_scene, measured with the
six-baseline WLS direction of the shipped 2x2 array at 30 mm.

Study A: three emitters, clustered per frame and then linked
  T1 1 MHz hopping over the band at 10 deg, T2 1 MHz CW at 3.05 GHz,
  T3 5 MHz hopping at -15 deg. 10 frames, 8 snapshots, 20 dB per bin,
  410 bins of 1.953 MHz, 6 draws.

  Per frame, how many detection clusters appear (three emitters):
    scene              frequency   direction     both
    distinct angles          3.0         3.0      3.2
    shared angle             3.0         2.0      3.2
  Frequency finds the three blocks but cannot say which emitter owns
  one when two of them share bins; direction merges the two that share
  an angle (2.0 clusters for three emitters).

  Then link the per-frame clusters over 10 frames:

    direction                               4.7      3.5     3.0/3     1.00
    direction+bandwidth                     5.8      3.3     3.0/3     1.00
    direction+bandwidth+cadence             5.8      3.2     3.0/3     1.00
    -- shared angle
    carrier                                21.2      1.0     1.0/3     1.00

    direction+bandwidth                     5.0      3.0     2.3/3     0.70
    direction+bandwidth+cadence             5.0      3.0     3.0/3     1.00
  A carrier-only link -- the frequency-hopping-network way -- leaves
  the hoppers as singletons: 20.7 tracks for three emitters, 1 of
  them recovered, because every hop is a new frequency.
  Direction and bandwidth hold (5.8 tracks, 3 of 3 recovered, purity
  1.00) because a hop cannot change either.
  Tracks includes fragments -- a frame whose per-bin direction falls
  outside the 4 deg gate starts a new track -- so the mature column is
  the one that matters.
  Two emitters with the SAME direction and the SAME bandwidth are put
  in one track by either rule (2.3 of 3 recovered, purity 0.70);
  cadence is the third invariant and it is enough (3.0 of 3, purity
  1.00), because one carrier is occupied in every frame and the other
  never repeats.

Study B: masking needs a position, not a fingerprint
  T1 hops at random over 410 bin positions of the band; the other two
  emitters live in it. Five ways to get T1 out of one frame of the
  display, scored on now / the next hop / what else survives.
  strategy                                now   next hop others kept
  replay the last hop                    100%      0.20%      100%
  extrapolate the hop plan               100%      0.20%      100%
    same, a sequential plan              100%    100.00%      100%
  mask the whole band                    100%       100%        0%
  null its direction                     100%       100%     see C
  Every strategy removes T1 from the frame you are looking at -- that is
  a detection, not a fingerprint, and it is free. Predicting the NEXT hop
  is the only thing a fingerprint could add, and for random hopping it
  is worth 0.20% (100% for a sequential plan). A band mask predicts
  nothing but takes every other emitter with it. A null needs no
  frequency prediction at all -- but study C measures what it costs,
  and on this aperture it is not selective in angle either.

Study C: three ways to remove a known emitter, and their price
  T1 at 10 deg, T2 at 10 + separation, equal power, 8 bins simulated,
  16 snapshots, 20 dB/bin. Directions come from a Bartlett scan on the
  effective manifold, so a filter can be accounted for by the estimator
  that knows it. First the cost of a null on an emitter that is NOT
  colliding with the removed one -- the pure cost of the filter:
                 shipped 2x2 (4 el)    3x3 at 30 mm (9 el)
  separation        power     error        power     error
  2 deg          -20.9 dB   82.0 deg     -19.5 dB   67.0 deg
  5 deg          -18.8 dB   85.0 deg     -16.6 dB   63.3 deg
  10 deg         -15.6 dB   90.0 deg     -12.1 dB   50.0 deg
  20 deg         -10.8 dB  100.0 deg      -7.1 dB   40.0 deg
  40 deg          -6.3 dB  120.0 deg      -2.8 dB   20.0 deg
  A 2x2 square at elevation 0 has only TWO distinct element phases --
  the phase depends on the y coordinate alone -- so it is a two-channel
  array. Nulling one direction there does not attenuate the neighbour,
  it rotates its phase by about 90 deg: the errors of 82-120 deg are
  not noise, they are the residual of a two-channel projection, and the
  neighbour's direction stops being measurable at any separation.
  With three elements per side the null is less brutal (-12.1 dB at 10
  deg) but the direction is still off by 50.0 deg, so it is a better
  filter, not a usable mask. Masking needs aperture this kit does not
  have; labelling needs nothing more than what it already measures.

  Now the collision itself: T1 and T2 on the SAME bin, 20 deg apart.
  what is done                   kept     T2 error    T1 residual
  nothing                       100%      9.75 deg         (none)
  notch                           0%    100.00 deg        removed
  null                          100%    100.00 deg     -1.2 dB
  cancel 0 dB                   100%    100.00 deg     -1.4 dB
  cancel 6 dB                   100%     25.42 deg     23.1 dB
  Nothing removed, the two sources are one detection and the direction
  lands between them (9.8 deg from T2 with 20 deg of separation): the
  collision is a limit of the data, not of the display. The notch is the
  only complete removal and T2 goes with it (kept 0%). The null and
  the cancellation keep the power (kept 100%) and destroy the
  direction (100 deg). Cancellation does remove T1 -- at 0 dB of gain
  error the residual is -1.4 dB, the noise floor -- but what is left of
  T2 is no longer a plane wave on this array, so its direction is gone.
  That is the real objection to masking: not that the emitter survives,
  but that removing it costs whoever else is in the bin the ability to
  be measured at all.
  Depth of the null on the emitter it is aimed at: 21 dB.

  Why the null cannot be a mask here: at elevation 0 the phase across
  this array depends on the y coordinate alone, so the 2x2 is a
  two-channel array and a null leaves one channel -- every direction
  maps onto the same residual, and no estimator can recover T2 from it.
  Measured with the estimator that ships in this repository, on nulled
  data, the plane-wave fit lands 130 deg from a T2 that was 40 deg away.
  A mask is therefore not available on this aperture: the workable
  removal is the notch, and the notch is exactly what deletes the
  co-channel emitter. That is the whole argument for labelling.

Study D: can two emitters be separated by clustering at all?
  two CW emitters, 6 draws per cell, joint (direction, frequency)
  features in one frame; solved when the two clusters match exactly.
  angle \ df         0 bin    1 bin    4 bin   20 bin
  1 deg                 0%      17%     100%     100%
  3 deg                 0%      83%     100%     100%
  6 deg                 0%     100%     100%     100%
  12 deg                0%     100%     100%     100%
  25 deg                0%     100%     100%     100%
  The cluster is the pair, so what matters is that one coordinate
  separates them. The same bin (df = 0) is not solvable at any angle:
  there is one detection and no second coordinate to split it -- that is
  the two-source MUSIC case of V07 11.2, not a clustering problem.
  Below about 3 deg direction alone stops working and a frequency
  offset rescues it; at 25 deg either coordinate is enough.

VERDICT
  To cluster the hopper you do not extract its fingerprint and mask it
  out of the spectrum. A mask is a destructive edit of the cube you are
  clustering and it needs the position you were about to measure; on
  this aperture it is also measured to be unusable (study C): the notch
  deletes whoever shares the bin, and the null rotates the neighbour's
  phase so badly that its direction stops being estimable. What
  clusters an emitter is what a hop cannot change. In order:
  1. per frame, take the detections and cluster them on (direction,
     bandwidth): 3.2 clusters per frame for three emitters. Frequency
     alone cannot say who owns a block when two share bins, direction
     alone merges two emitters that share an angle (2.0 for three).
  2. link those clusters over frames on the same pair: 3 of 3 emitters
     at purity 1.00, because direction and bandwidth hold while the
     carrier moves. A carrier-only link -- the hopping-network way --
     recovers 1 of 3: the hopper becomes 21 singletons.
  3. when two emitters share both, cadence is the third invariant: same
     direction, same bandwidth, 2 of 3 recovered at purity 0.70, and
     adding cadence gives 3 of 3 at purity 1.00.
  4. masking buys nothing here, and a fingerprint buys one thing only:
     predicting the next hop, worth 0.2% for random hopping against
     100% for a sequential plan. Clustering never needs it.
  The map in study E is the picture to hand to an operator: one row per
  emitter, the hopper as scattered blocks along the row, the static one
  as a fixed column, and a shared cell marked. 1 of 25 cells are shared
  in this sample -- those are the limit described in V07 14.6, and they
  are a property of the data, not of the display.
